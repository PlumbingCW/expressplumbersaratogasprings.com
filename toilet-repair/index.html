A toilet that runs, hisses or refills on its own

A running toilet wastes water all day and often sounds like a faint hiss from the bathroom. Almost every case comes from one of three parts: the flapper that seals the tank bottom, the fill valve that refills the tank, or the float that tells the fill valve when to stop.

A quick dye test finds the leaky one. Drop a little food coloring into the tank, wait 15 minutes without flushing, and look in the bowl. Color in the bowl means the flapper is letting water through, and no color points toward the fill valve or float.

When an overflowing toilet is an emergency

A toilet that spills onto the floor can soak subfloor and ceiling drywall below it in minutes. If the water is clean tank water, the cleanup is simple. If it is bowl water, treat it as contaminated and keep children and pets out of the room.

  1. Stop the waterLift the tank lid and push the flapper down, or close the oval valve behind the toilet clockwise until it stops.
  2. Do not flush againEach flush sends more water toward a bowl that has nowhere to drain.
  3. Check the other drainsIf the tub or a floor drain gurgles, the blockage is probably in the line past the toilet. Shut off water use in the house and call right away.
  4. Contain the floorPut towels at the door, lift rugs and keep power strips and cords off the wet area.
  5. Call the 24/7 lineTell the dispatcher what is overflowing and whether more than one fixture is affected.

Water at the base: wax ring, closet bolts and the flange

A toilet sits on a wax ring that seals the bowl outlet to the drain flange in the floor. When that seal fails, water seeps out at the base each time you flush. You may notice a soft spot in the floor, a stain on the ceiling below, or a toilet that rocks when you sit.

Not every wet base is a failed ring. Condensation, a loose tank bolt or a worn supply line can also drip to the floor, so we check those before we pull the toilet.

Once the bowl is lifted, we inspect the flange. A cracked or corroded flange, or one that sits below the finished floor, will not hold a new ring in place. We repair it with a flange ring or replacement piece, set a fresh seal and tighten the closet bolts evenly. Rotted subfloor is carpentry outside our scope, so we point you to a contractor first.

A clogged toilet that will not clear

Start with a flange plunger, not the flat cup type. Seal the cup over the outlet and push slowly, so you move the clog instead of splashing water. If three or four rounds do nothing, a closet auger reaches the trap bend where most solid blockages sit.

Stop plunging and call if

  • Both toilets and the tub gurgle or back up together
  • The bowl drains slowly after every flush, even with no paper
  • A plunger and auger both fail to move the water level
  • A child's toy or other hard object went down the bowl
  • The same toilet clogs again within a week or two

One clog is usually a toilet problem. Repeat clogs, or a backup in other fixtures, point to the line behind it, and our drain cleaning team cables the branch or main line and uses a camera to find roots or a sag.

Supply valves and supply lines under the tank

The small oval or round valve on the wall is the toilet's shutoff, and it is the part most people test only in an emergency. Old valves seize, drip at the stem, or turn but do not stop the flow. A braided supply line can also kink, corrode at the nut or burst at the crimp.

We replace the valve and line together with a quarter-turn valve that is easier to close in a hurry. If a stuck valve will not shut off at all, close the main water valve for the house. Our faucet and sink repair page covers the same valves under sinks.

Installing a new toilet, step by step

Replacement makes sense when the porcelain is cracked, the bowl stains or leaks internally, or repairs would cost close to a new unit. Before you buy, measure the rough-in, which is the distance from the finished wall to the center of the floor flange. Common sizes are 10, 12 and 14 inches, and a wrong match leaves the tank touching the wall.

  1. Shut off and drainWe close the supply valve, flush the tank and sponge out the remaining water.
  2. Pull the old toiletWe disconnect the supply, remove the closet bolts and lift the bowl, then scrape off the old wax.
  3. Inspect the flangeThe flange must be solid, level with the floor and free of cracks before anything new goes on.
  4. Set and secureA new seal goes on, the bowl is pressed straight down and the bolts are snugged in alternating turns.
  5. Connect and testWe attach the new supply line and flush several times while checking the base and the tank for drips.

What affects the price of toilet work

Repair or replacement

A flapper or fill valve is a small parts job. A wax ring swap takes longer, and a full toilet installation adds the unit itself.

Flange and floor condition

A cracked flange or a wet subfloor adds repair work before a toilet can be reset.

Parts and fixtures

Standard tank parts cost far less than a one-piece or elongated toilet with a comfort-height bowl.

Clog location

A bowl clog is quick. A clog in the line behind the toilet moves into drain cleaning.

Time of day

Nights, weekends and holidays are answered the same way. Ask for the clear price before work starts.

A note on insurance

Homeowners insurance typically covers sudden water damage from an overflow or supply line failure, but not the toilet repair itself or damage from long-term seepage. Photograph the damage and read your policy before you start cleanup.
